Understanding Dementia

Dementia types & symptoms

Dementia is an umbrella term for a range of conditions — each with different causes, progressions, and care needs. Understanding which type your loved one has helps you plan more effectively.

01

Alzheimer's Disease

The most common form of dementia. Alzheimer's is a progressive brain disease that gradually destroys memory, thinking skills, and eventually the ability to carry out basic daily tasks. Symptoms typically appear in the mid-60s in late-onset cases.

Early stages often involve forgetting recently learned information and asking the same questions repeatedly. As the disease progresses, symptoms become more severe, eventually requiring full-time care.

Learn more at Alzheimer's Association ↗
02

Vascular Dementia

Caused by reduced blood flow to the brain — often following a stroke or series of mini-strokes (TIAs). Symptoms may appear suddenly after a vascular event and can vary significantly depending on the area of the brain affected.

Unlike Alzheimer's, vascular dementia often presents with more pronounced problems with planning, decision-making, and speed of thinking rather than memory loss in the early stages.

Learn more at Alzheimer's Society ↗
03

Lewy Body Dementia

A common but frequently misdiagnosed type, caused by deposits of alpha-synuclein (Lewy bodies) in nerve cells. LBD combines cognitive symptoms with physical features similar to Parkinson's disease, as well as vivid visual hallucinations.

People with LBD may have days of near-normal cognition alternating with days of significant confusion. This variability can be confusing for families and is a key diagnostic feature.

Learn more at LBDA ↗
04

Frontotemporal Dementia

FTD affects the frontal and temporal lobes — areas responsible for personality, behaviour, and language. It is one of the most common causes of early-onset dementia, often affecting people in their 40s, 50s, and 60s.

Unlike Alzheimer's, memory may remain relatively intact in early FTD. The most common early symptoms are personality changes, loss of empathy, or difficulty with language — which can be deeply distressing for families.

Learn more at AFTD ↗
Free to Contact · No Referral Needed

Florida & national dementia support organisations

These organisations offer guidance, helplines, carer support, and research updates. They are all free to contact and do not require a referral.

Alzheimer's Association

The leading voluntary health organization in Alzheimer's care, support, and research. Their 24/7 helpline (800-272-3900) connects you with dementia specialists at any hour. They also offer local chapter support groups and a comprehensive online resource library.

Visit alz.org ↗

Caregiver Action Network

A non-profit supporting the more than 90 million family caregivers across the United States. CAN offers peer support, educational resources, and advocacy programs specifically designed for unpaid family caregivers managing a loved one's care.

Visit caregiveraction.org ↗

Florida Dept. of Elder Affairs

Florida's state agency for aging services, connecting residents to local services through SHINE counselors, Aging Resource Centers, and Area Agencies on Aging. DOEA can help you navigate state-funded support programs at no cost.

Visit elderaffairs.org ↗
Plan Early

Legal & financial planning for Florida dementia families

Putting legal and financial arrangements in place early — ideally while the person with dementia can still participate in decisions — is one of the most important steps families can take.

This is general information, not legal or financial advice. Florida law has specific requirements — please speak with an elder-law attorney or a SHINE counselor about your family's situation.

A Durable Power of Attorney allows a trusted person to make financial and legal decisions if your loved one loses capacity. It must be set up while the person still has mental capacity — waiting too long can require expensive court intervention.

Medicare covers many medical services but has limited coverage for long-term care. Medicaid can cover more — including some home care — for those who qualify financially. Florida Medicaid's HCBS waivers may fund in-home care services.

If your loved one is a veteran, significant benefits may be available — including the Aid & Attendance pension, which can provide meaningful financial support for in-home care costs. The VA benefits system is complex but worth navigating.

A healthcare advance directive (living will) and a healthcare surrogate designation allow your loved one to specify wishes for medical care and name someone to make healthcare decisions on their behalf. Florida law has specific requirements.

From Families Who Have Been There

Practical dementia caregiver tips

These are the things experienced carers consistently say they wished they had known sooner. Not medical advice — just honest, practical guidance from families who have been where you are.

01

Accept help when it is offered — and ask for it when it is not.

Carrying everything alone is not a virtue; it is a path to burnout. You do not need to earn the right to support.

02

Establish and protect simple daily routines.

Consistency and predictability reduce confusion and agitation significantly in many people with dementia. Small rituals carry enormous weight.

03

Focus on the emotion, not the facts.

When your loved one becomes distressed — even about something that did not happen — acknowledge how they feel rather than correcting them. The feeling is real, even when the memory is not.

04

Adapt the home environment early.

Simple changes (removing trip hazards, adding nightlights, labelling cupboards) can meaningfully reduce accidents and stress for everyone in the household.

05

Keep a care journal.

A brief daily record helps you spot patterns, communicate with medical teams, and recognise how things change over time in a way that memory alone cannot.

06

Understand that grief starts before loss.

Anticipatory grief — mourning the person you are losing while they are still here — is real, valid, and very common. Seek support for yourself, not just for your loved one.

07

Don't underestimate the value of music.

For many people with dementia, music from earlier in life remains accessible and deeply moving long after other memories fade. Use it deliberately and often.

08

Connect with other caregivers.

Peer support — from someone who truly understands — is different from anything a professional can provide. Local support groups and online communities are worth the time.
